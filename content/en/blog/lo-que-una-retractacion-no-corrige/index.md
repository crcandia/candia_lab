On October 23, IEEE retracted at least 1,417 conference papers, according to a count by Nature’s news team (2026). A study had linked those papers to advertisements selling authorship on social media (Abalkina et al., 2026).

Science needs institutions willing to acknowledge that work published under their name no longer deserves the same trust.

But consider someone who downloaded one of those papers months earlier. They saved it on their computer, included it in a review, and shared it with a colleague. The notice now appears on the publisher’s website. The copy in their folder is still the same.

How far did the correction travel?

Anton Kühberger and his colleagues compared thousands of retracted papers with similar publications that had not been retracted. Average annual citations to the retracted papers fell from almost five to two. In the comparison group, they rose from about four to five (Kühberger et al., 2022).

That is a substantial decline, but the average does not tell us what authors are doing when they continue to cite those papers.

A citation might discuss fraud, trace the history of an error, or explain why a finding is no longer reliable. In those cases, continuing to cite the paper helps correct knowledge. To assess what is happening, we need to read how the paper is being used.

Tzu-Kun Hsiao and Jodi Schneider did exactly that in biomedicine. They examined 13,252 passages containing citations made after a retraction. Only 5.4% mentioned that the paper had been retracted (Hsiao & Schneider, 2022).

The missing warning does not prove that every other citation endorsed invalid findings. It does leave readers without information relevant to deciding how much to trust the source.

Retractions can also have different grounds. An authorship irregularity does not, on its own, establish that every finding is false. That is why preserving the notice and explaining what happened matters. Deleting the paper would prevent scrutiny. Keeping it available without the warning would leave the correction incomplete.

This question is close to my research on collective memory: what happens to knowledge when it loses the support it once had. A retracted paper can retain readers, citations, and copies. The warning needs to reach those who read it, cite it, or keep the file for later use.

Artificial intelligence adds another possible destination for these texts.

A preprint published in October estimated that peS2o, a scientific training corpus, contained about 12,200 retracted papers. In the sample examined, 79% had no mention of the retraction (Gracia, 2026). The study has not been peer reviewed, and its checks relied mainly on AI. It does not establish that those documents caused erroneous model responses.

If a text enters training data without the warning, or is retracted later, how does the system receive that update?

A search engine can update a document’s record and display a warning the next time someone finds it. An already trained model requires another intervention: changing the original corpus does not automatically change its responses. It can consult updated sources when answering, but we still need to check whether it recognizes the retraction and changes how it uses the paper.

Crossref publicly distributes Retraction Watch data, updated every working day. These records can be queried automatically to connect a publication with its retraction notice (Crossref, n.d.).

A reference manager could alert us before we submit a manuscript. A repository could update the records for the copies it holds. An AI assistant searching for papers could check their status before presenting them as evidence. Those preparing training data could record when they checked that information and how they handled the affected papers.

After a retraction, we should examine whether readers encounter the warning, whether new citations acknowledge the problem, and whether systems consulting the literature change their answers when appropriate. None of those checks requires every reference to the paper to disappear.

The publisher can issue the notice, but libraries, repositories, and tool providers need to incorporate the update. Those of us doing research also have a task: to check our references and explain why we retain a citation to a retracted paper. Responsibility is shared among those who publish, preserve, and use the literature.

Let us return to the person who downloaded the paper before the notice appeared. When they open that copy to finish their review, they will find the same pages. Before using it as evidence, they need to know that the paper was retracted, why, and what that means for the claim they were preparing to make.
